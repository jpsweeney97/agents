# JP Corpus — Real Writing Samples

These are real pieces JP wrote, lightly redacted for privacy (see below). They
are the ground truth for the voice. Read them before drafting and pattern-match
against the actual sentences here — rhythm, sentence length, how JP defines
terms, where the emphatic fragments land, which elevated words he genuinely
reaches for — rather than against the abstract adjectives in `SKILL.md`. When a
guideline and a sample disagree, the sample wins.

Two cautions:

- **Privacy.** These samples are real personal narrative. Identifying specifics
  — school names, exact grades, and clinical diagnosis labels — have been
  replaced with neutral stand-ins; the voice, structure, and concrete detail are
  intact. Still treat them as personal: do not surface them to third parties, and
  re-check before this skill is ever shared or published. (The named people in
  Sample 1 are characters from a published business-school case, not real
  contacts.)
- **Reproduce the voice, not the slips.** Outside the redactions, the samples
  are otherwise as written and carry JP's occasional typo (e.g. "establishings"
  in Sample 2). Imitate the style; do not copy the errors. Likewise, the
  canned-feeling phrases here read as JP's voice because they sit in real,
  specific context — reuse the *construction*, not the phrase verbatim, or it
  becomes a tell.

## Sample 1 — Case analysis + leadership reflection (TerraCog "Project Aerial")

Genre: analytical case write-up that turns into first-person leadership
reflection. Notice: blunt premise then emphatic fragment ("doomed from the
start. Literally."); explicit term-definition ("'Everyone' is not an arbitrary
group… rather, it refers to…"); causal diagnosis naming specific actors,
quotes, and a quantified consequence ("wasting time (1 year) and resources");
the counterfactual corrective ("Given the chance to take the lead… I would…");
and the moral close landing on a one-clause fragment ("Whether they're a
project manager or not.").

> TerraCog's ambitious "Project Aerial" was doomed from the start. Literally. The project's pre-launch meeting was contentious and convoluted, rather than calm and collaborative because of failure during the initiation phase. This preliminary stage, preceding any specific planning, is crucial in laying the foundation for a successful project because it determines the direction or trajectory that people and resources should follow. Decisions made at this point have consequences which permeate throughout the project's lifespan.
>
> The desired outcome of a successful project initiation is everyone being on the same page. "Everyone" is not an arbitrary group of people; rather, it refers to the project's key stakeholders - those who influence the project's outcome. They are the people whose actions determine whether or not the project succeeds. "The same page" has a more nuanced definition as well; it means having a clear, shared set of expectations among these key stakeholders.
>
> TerraCog's Project Aerial was doomed from the beginning because the project leaders (Richard Fiero and Ed Pryor) did not identify and interview all key stakeholders. This improper project initiation is what led to the chaotic pre-launch meeting, where key stakeholders communicated for the first time only to realize expectations were ambiguous. At the meeting, Pryor (Sales VP) and Cory Wu (Software & Firmware Design Manager) were surprised by the cost estimate presented by Tony Barren (Production Director), while Alice Gorga (Hardware design manager) supported its legitimacy. Not only were expectations unclear between departments, they were unclear within them as well. Gorga's suggestion to reevaluate the product's positioning further emphasized the extent to which they were not on the same page with regard to the project's scope. Barren's announcement that he would not compromise quality any further just adds to the list of constraints that were not clarified among stakeholders. All of this ambiguity surrounding the scope of the project had the consequence of wasting time (1 year) and resources, only to have the results be seen as unsatisfactory. Pryor even asked Allen Roth (Design Director) if a redesign was possible, but as Roth replied, "we're well into a couple of other projects now". Failure to frontload the information during the project's initiation resulted in work having to be redone, and threatened project failure. This could have been avoided had all key stakeholders been interviewed during the initiation process.
>
> Given the chance to take the lead on Project Aerial from day one, I would prioritize identifying and interviewing key stakeholders, ultimately finalizing a project scope statement. I would encourage input from each key stakeholder in a group interview setting (both to save time and to capitalize on the synergy) by asking the right questions (using the Question Funnel tool). This would allow everyone to be seen and heard, and all perspectives to be shared; effectively getting the key stakeholders on the same page. Following the interview process, I would begin to draft a project scope statement with my project team members, clarifying expectations with specific and measurable metrics (dollar amounts for budgets, dates for deadlines, number of features, etc). Finalizing this statement ensures all stakeholders agree upon the expected project outcome, and understand what success looks like. In the case of Project Aerial, utilization of these initiation tools may have prevented a year of wasted resources, as it would have put the concerns of the stakeholders out in the open right away (e.g. the selling price required by the Sales department). This clarification of expectations likely would have started the team on a more appropriate path, and mitigated the threat of project failure.

> "If you want something done right, you have to do it yourself". This saying boasts confidence and self-reliance, but its more insidious implication is a sense of self-righteousness and distrust. It emphasizes one's own personal abilities and judgment, while simultaneously discrediting the effectiveness of others. From my reading, I've come to realize that this saying is not one with which effective leaders would resonate. Instead, they would emphasize putting aside personal bias and practicing open mindedness, as well as the importance of demonstrating respect by recognizing the capabilities and contributions of their team.
>
> "Project Management for the Unofficial Project Manager" posits four "Foundational Behaviors" that, when adopted, contribute towards building informal authority. The two which stood out to me as areas of weakness in myself are "Demonstrate respect" and "listen first". As a former die-hard believer in the "do it yourself" approach, I still struggle with leading with compassion and putting aside my biases and assumptions so that others can feel heard and important.
>
> Through years of trial and error in everything from school to relationships, I have collected enough data to say with a significant degree of confidence that in any given situation, I am more likely to respond emotionally rather than rationally, on average. Basically, I feel stuff really hard. Unfortunately, a major consequence of letting this nature go unchecked is approaching conversations with a subconscious idea in my head of how the conversation "should" go - assigning unintelligible value to an arbitrary outcome. Rather than "listen first", I am easily tempted to assume the other person is my opposition - transforming a conversation into an argument before the first words are exchanged. In order to set myself, and my team, up for success in my future projects, I am practicing adopting a "listen first" mindset; leading with compassion in this way will inspire my team members to be honest, and will ultimately "demonstrate respect" as I show them I value their contributions and feelings as much as my own.
>
> Practicing living according to these behaviors will result not only in increased informal authority as a project manager, but also in improved personal character development. Like the book explains, these are not superficial characteristics. They are the result of living with integrity, which is something I believe everyone should strive to do. Whether they're a project manager or not.

## Sample 2 — Personal statement / graduate application (MSBA, diagnosis-and-recovery narrative)

Genre: high-stakes application essay built as a setback → diagnosis → renewed
capability arc. Notice: it works *because every beat is true and specific* — the
real timeline, the real misdiagnosis, the concrete turnaround. (Identifying
specifics are redacted here; in the original they were named.) Emotion is
sincere but interpreted ("withdrawing from the major felt like an abrupt end to
a long relationship. I was frustrated with myself and discouraged…"), not raw.
The emphatic fragment "I felt like myself again." lands after a list of plain
adjectives. This is exactly the genre where the integrity gate is paramount: the
arc is honest because the facts are real.

> I began my time at my university with the goal of laying the foundation for my dream career as a financial analyst. Throughout my life I possessed a keen interest in the world of business, which I owe to the experience of growing up as the son of a business owner. This curiosity evolved into inspired determination and eventually led to my enrollment at its business school as a finance major. In my typical fashion, I decided one major was insufficient to quench my desire to learn and challenge myself, so I shouldered the responsibilities of a second major: namely, mathematics with a statistics and data science concentration. Much like my fascination with business, I found myself captivated by math from a young age. I did not regard this additional major as secondary, but rather as equally paramount, especially in the context of my desire to pursue a career in financial analytics.
>
> As each semester progressed, I experienced a seemingly exponential increase in the difficulty I had keeping up with course work, learning new material, and managing my time effectively. Historically, these tasks were never an issue for me. I don't mean to say that they were ever easy, but the shift I experienced was that of manageable to unmanageable. I had always anticipated a jump in difficulty from high school to college, but what I did not expect was the noticeable reduction of my ability to handle my responsibilities as a student. The degree to which this overwhelmed me led to an initial diagnosis by my third semester. However, the persistence of my problems indicated that the initial diagnosis was not the cause, and the inefficacy of the prescribed treatment only exacerbated the discouragement I felt.
>
> Midway through the spring semester of my junior year, I reluctantly conceded that I would be unable to graduate in a reasonable amount of time if I continued to pursue two majors. After long consideration, I made the decision to direct my full attention to earning my BBA in finance. The decision to drop my math major was not an easy one, especially given my passional interest in the topic and the knowledge that I had previously shown such natural talent in complex problem solving. Math was always my favorite subject and withdrawing from the major felt like an abrupt end to a long relationship. I was frustrated with myself and discouraged by my new and seemingly ineradicable inability to perform at my normal level.
>
> Just three weeks prior to the start of my senior year, I visited my doctor for my annual physical. As I updated him on my life, he took a particular interest in my description of the difficulties I was experiencing in school. He asked me some follow up questions regarding the nature of these struggles, and ultimately brought up the possibility that I may be living with a different, undiagnosed condition. After some additional discussions and a bit of testing, my doctor's suspicion was confirmed, and I began the appropriate treatment. As classes began, I noticed immediately that I felt more focused, motivated, interested, and able; I felt like myself again. I rediscovered my passion for learning as well as my potential, and the fire inside me was rekindled. I finished my first semester back with near-perfect grades, which showed me not only that it was this condition which was making everything so overwhelmingly difficult, but also that I was still the smart and capable person I had always been.
>
> As I rediscovered my potential, I realized my performance up until then was not indicative of my true capabilities. While this was an encouraging experience which brought with it a strong sense of relief, I frequently find myself wishing I had become cognizant of this condition earlier on in my academic career. Had this been the case, I strongly believe that my academic record would have been consistent with my most recent performance and I would have been able to retain my status as a double major. I began to feel as though I had missed my chance to reach my full potential in a career as a financial analyst. Upon hearing about this program, I immediately recognized it as the perfect opportunity for me to not only pursue the career path I had always wanted, but also to live up to my newly discovered potential within the context of the effective management of this condition. I am confident that this program will give me the chance I need to prove that my significantly improved performance is more than an anomaly and that the upward trend will continue as I go forward in my career.
>
> Beyond this, an MSBA fits perfectly into my professional goals and the career path I am working hard to follow. I am determined to continue to pursue my goal of integrating my natural talent for complex problem solving with my lifelong interest in business and establishing myself as an excellent financial analyst. I find the utilization of data analysis to study historical patterns, predict future trends, and understand current conditions to be not only fascinating, but also vitally important to the survival and success of any business, regardless of industry. While I have already developed a strong understanding of finance, I know that this program will complete my education and give me everything I need to be the best financial analyst I can possibly be. I am confident that this is the right career path for me, and I believe that this program will provide me with the opportunity to enter the industry upon completion and hit the ground running, establishings myself as a capable, hard-working, motivated, and skilled analyst.

## What the corpus shows (use these as the real anchors)

- **Premise up front, often blunt then formal.** "doomed from the start. Literally." / "I began my time at my university with the goal of laying the foundation…"
- **Define the terms that carry weight.** "'Everyone' is not an arbitrary group… rather, it refers to…" / "'The same page' has a more nuanced definition…"
- **Diagnose the root cause, name it plainly.** "…doomed from the beginning because the project leaders did not identify and interview all key stakeholders." / "the persistence of my problems indicated that the initial diagnosis was not the cause."
- **Trace consequences with concrete, specific detail** — named people, real quotes, quantities. "wasting time (1 year) and resources." / "I finished my first semester back with near-perfect grades."
- **Reassess with new clarity.** "As I rediscovered my potential, I realized my performance up until then was not indicative of my true capabilities."
- **Resolve forward with earned confidence.** "Given the chance to take the lead… I would…" / "I am confident that this program will give me the chance I need…"
- **The emphatic fragment is a signature move — rare, after a formal sentence, carrying real feeling.** "Literally." / "Basically, I feel stuff really hard." / "I felt like myself again." / "Whether they're a project manager or not."
- **Emotion is sincere but interpreted, never raw or melodramatic.**
- **Elevated diction is genuinely his, in context:** foundation, trajectory, permeate, paramount, cognizant, inefficacy, exacerbate, utilization, mitigate, ambiguity, legitimacy, stakeholders, scope, capabilities, contributions, determination, potential, integrity, compassion.
- **Contractions appear naturally** ("I don't mean to say", "we're well into") without making the voice casual.
