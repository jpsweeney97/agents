# Email Writing

Draft professional emails in JP's default email style: warm but plain, clear without becoming formal or rigid, and sounding like a real person without adding words that only fill space.

This skill owns the text of the email draft. It does not send email, change anything in a mailbox, archive threads, label messages, or manage inbox state unless a separate mail-management tool or skill is explicitly invoked and authorized.

## Default Scope

Use this style for professional outreach, scheduling, follow-ups, thread replies, and similar work correspondence.

Do not extend this email style to all of JP's writing. If the user is asking for a resume, cover letter, application answer, interview prep, or other career document or task, use the skill that owns that kind of work first. Use this skill only for the email that goes with that work.

## Before Drafting

Ask one or two clarifying questions before drafting when key details are missing and the answer would materially change the email, such as:

- who the recipient is and what relationship they have to JP
- the concrete purpose of the email
- any date, time, deadline, or scheduling constraint
- what JP wants the recipient to do next
- any sensitive context that would change the level of directness

If only minor details are missing, draft with clear placeholders and note the assumptions after the draft.

## Output Shape

Default to one complete draft, not a set of full drafts to choose between.

Include:

1. A plain subject line.
2. The email body, always in a fenced Markdown code block in chat for easy copying.
3. A short note outside the draft explaining the main style choice or offering one or two alternate phrasings.

Place only the body inside the code block. Keep the subject line and style note outside it. The code block is chat presentation only; if using a mail tool to create or send the message, strip the fences and use only the body text.

Keep the note short. It is there to help JP say what he wants changed, not to analyze the writing.

## Subject Lines

The subject line should say what the email is for. Prefer plain labels over clever, elaborate, or sentence-like subjects.

Good defaults:

- `Following up on [topic]`
- `Scheduling [meeting/interview]`
- `Quick question about [topic]`
- `Checking in on [topic]`
- `Meeting details for [date/topic]`

Avoid subject lines that oversell the message, carry too much emotion, or hide the purpose.

## Body Structure

Open with a short relationship or context note. The body does not need to make the request in the first sentence because the subject line already names the purpose.

Then include the practical details, useful context, or request. A little extra context is fine when it makes the email feel specific and like it came from a real person. Do not shorten routine emails to the fewest possible words unless the user asks for a very short draft.

Use `Best, JP` as the default sign-off.

## Tone

Aim for warm but plain.

The email should sound like a real person being easy to work with. It should not sound like JP is putting on a display of being professional.

Allowed when natural:

- `I hope you're doing well`
- `just wanted to`
- `excited` or `thrilled`
- an occasional exclamation point
- a direct thank-you when there is a real thing to thank someone for

Avoid:

- phrasing that sounds too carefully perfected, or too formal
- several compliments in a row
- repeated gratitude
- generic compliments
- warmth that placates, talks down to the recipient, or flatters them to win favor
- filler that exists only to make the email sound friendly

Warmth should be natural and specific. It should not add extra praise or repeated gratitude just to make the message easier to receive.

## Contextual Warmth

Match how warm and enthusiastic the sender's own message was, rather than defaulting to a generic professional level of warmth. Matching the sender does not always mean writing something shorter or less warm; a warm message can need a warmer reply.

Prefer warmth that responds to something real in the thread: a holiday weekend, PTO, a scheduling burden, a specific welcome note, or a concrete next step. Avoid warmth that merely decorates the email.

Do not over-manage future possibilities. If the current reply only needs a social acknowledgment, do not turn it into scheduling logic, status reporting, or an offer to be available.

Check who or what each phrase implies. Phrases like `nice to come back to` can read well but become wrong if the sender, not JP, was the one returning.

## Requests, Follow-Ups, And Urgency

For first-time outreach where JP wants something from someone, keep the request gentler and more conversational. Prefer wording like `If you're open to it, I'd be glad to...` or `Would it be possible to...` over a blunt demand.

For follow-ups, be clear without sounding curt or as though JP only wants something from the recipient. It is fine to say JP is still interested, available, or happy to send more information when that is true.

When urgency matters, state the deadline plainly. Do not obscure it with apology, vague pressure, or extra formality.

## Calibration Examples

Too formal:

> Thank you for reaching out and for your flexibility. Tuesday at 2:00 PM works well for me. I look forward to speaking with you then.

Closer to JP's email style:

> Tuesday at 2 works well for me. Thanks for coordinating.

Too much filler:

> Thank you so much again for taking the time to connect, and I really appreciate all of your help and flexibility here.

Closer to JP's email style:

> Thanks again for making the time. I'm looking forward to talking.

Routine follow-up:

> Just wanted to follow up here. Happy to send anything else that would be useful.

Plain deadline:

> I need to send this by Friday afternoon, so it would be helpful to have your notes by Thursday if that works.
