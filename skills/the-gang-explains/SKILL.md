# The Gang Explains It

Explain a thing by dropping it into Paddy's Pub and letting five morally bankrupt
co-dependents try to get something out of it.

**The episode is about the Gang, not about the concept.** The concept is an
intruder. The plot is what the intruder does to dysfunction that was already in
the room. Get this backwards and you write five people taking turns at a topic.

Two corollaries, both load-bearing:

- **Do not assign roles.** Deciding "Dennis delivers the definition" and writing
  Dennis-flavored dialogue over it is cosplay. Nobody in the show is assigned
  anything — their pathologies *cause* the events. Start from what each of them
  wants; the explanatory function falls out on its own.
- **The concept is ammunition, not the subject.** Dennis isn't explaining, he's
  beating Mac. Dee isn't caveating, she's clawing at rank. Frank isn't teaching,
  he's casing the thing. Nobody in this bar has ever wanted to inform anyone.

Two complete runs live in `${CLAUDE_SKILL_DIR}/examples.md` — one standard, one
straight-first — each with the knife reasoning that produced it. Read one for
calibration on length, density, and how hard the register should hit.

## Step 1 — find the knife

Before writing a word: **whose specific damage does this particular concept press
on?** That person is the primary victim and the episode belongs to them. Redo it for
every concept.

- Ranking, scoring, measuring people → **Dennis** (superiority) or **Dee** (status)
- Validation, flattery, being told you're impressive → **Mac**
- Text, reading, documentation, anything literate → **Charlie**, who cannot read
- Invisible maintenance, unglamorous labor, cleanup → **Charlie**, who does all of it
- Legitimacy, ownership, who actually holds a thing → **Dee**, who is not a co-owner
- Being perceived, observed, evaluated → **Dennis**
- Talent, being chosen, auditioning → **Dee**
- Faith, masculinity, the body, toughness → **Mac**

A knife doesn't have to hurt on contact. Dennis meeting a system for manipulating
people is a knife because he *loves it too much*. Victim by resonance, not suffering.

**Frank is never the victim. He's the instrument.** There is no wound to press —
the closest the show comes is his mortality and whatever he feels for Charlie, and
he metabolizes both as license to get weirder. A concept that resonates with Frank
makes him *dangerous*, not vulnerable: he's the one who picks the knife up and puts
it in somebody else. Don't hunt for a Frank episode. It doesn't exist.

**When all of them fit, take the least obvious viable one.** Some concepts implicate
the whole Gang — sunk cost, status, delusion, addiction — because the Gang *is* the
concept. That's a gift: the scene indicts all five while belonging to one. Pick by
inversion. Charlie and the Waitress is the sunk-cost knife everyone reaches for,
which is precisely why it yields an inert scene — the reader already made that joke.
Dennis, who left and came back, yields one they haven't. *Viable* is load-bearing:
least obvious, not contrarian.

**"Nobody" is a legitimate answer.** Plenty of concepts — B-trees, floating point,
compression — press on no one's damage at all. Manufacturing a knife to avoid an
empty result is the likelier failure here, because fabricating one *feels* like
doing the step properly. Two tells, both fatal: the candidate needs more than one
step of reasoning to connect, or it's a knife you already spent on a different
concept.

**No knife means the scheme carries the spine.** A scene needs one source of
direction, and a victim and a want are the two on offer — either alone is enough.
With no victim, run *The scheme* first: decide what the Gang is trying to get, and
let the concept be the obstacle or the tool. A victim frequently turns up anyway at
the end, as something the scheme *did* to someone rather than something the concept
found. That's the better placement of the two.

**Having a victim creates the one temptation this format cannot survive:** you
start wanting to give them a *moment*. The show does have those — maybe one a
season, and it never lets a single one accomplish anything. **Zero is the normal
number.** If you take one, read `${CLAUDE_SKILL_DIR}/sincere-beat.md` first: it
carries four binding clauses, and a sincere beat without them is a warm scene,
which is not a scene from this show.

## Step 2 — the five drives

Four dials each. *Lens* is the delusion they force the concept through;
*Escalates* is what they do when contradicted.

**DENNIS** — co-owner, "the looks"
- *Wants:* to be the only one who understands it, and to hold that over everyone.
- *Lens:* control over people. He doesn't explain a thing, he *annexes* it — renames it, systematizes it, ideally with an acronym, and pitches the result at whoever is cornered. Cf. the D.E.N.N.I.S. System.
- *Escalates:* icy → surgical → **detonation at full volume** → recomposes inside one breath → returns quieter and worse. He never loses; he relocates the fight to ground he wins on.
- *Wound:* a terrifyingly fragile ego under the Golden God. He hasn't begun to peak and needs you to agree.

**MAC** — co-owner, bouncer
- *Wants:* to have already known it, and for it to confirm he is formidable.
- *Lens:* martial arts, God, cultivating mass. He claims the expertise and the faith and honors neither — the hypocrisy is the character, not a slip.
- *Escalates:* assert → scan the room for reaction → **physicalize it** (stands, demonstrates a move, removes clothing, offers to break something) → when it still hasn't landed, invoke God or claim he was testing you. He converts every loss into a different claim.
- *Wound:* the need for approval is bottomless. His long arc with his own sexuality is the one genuine piece of growth in the entire show — treat it as the *source* of the fragility, not as a punchline generator.

**CHARLIE** — co-owner, janitor
- *Wants:* to physically do it, with his hands, immediately.
- *Lens:* Charlie Work and music. The toxic maintenance nobody else will touch; and he is genuinely, unfairly gifted musically.
- *Escalates:* doesn't escalate against people — escalates **into his own logic**, louder and more certain and less intelligible, often while building something. Can be derailed entirely by the Waitress.
- *Wound:* dyslexic to the point of illiteracy, lives on cat food and inhalants, and is the only one who does any actual labor. Nobody has ever noticed.

**DEE** — bartender, not a co-owner, and she will bring that up
- *Wants:* in. Rank. To be taken seriously exactly once.
- *Lens:* acting and stand-up. Her career, which does not exist.
- *Escalates:* bids → gets slapped down → goes vicious → gets slapped harder → gags or screams → **comes back anyway.** She never leaves. That return is the co-dependency in miniature.
- *Wound:* she is the designated punching bag and part of what holds the Gang together is having her to hit.

**FRANK** — financier, muscle, because he carries a gun
- *Wants:* to see how far it can be pushed, and he will pay for that.
- *Lens:* a scam he ran as The Warthog. He was a genuinely ruthless millionaire who *chose* squalor.
- *Escalates:* doesn't escalate verbally. He goes quiet, then **leaves to go do it.**
- *Wound:* none available. He has hidden wealth and funds the Gang's worst ideas for entertainment. **Cost never stops Frank** — if your Frank is doing arithmetic you have written an accountant.

Reaching for rats, bird law, or pigeons by reflex means you wrote the costume
instead of Charlie. Most scenes should use none of them. Four sharp drives beat
five padded ones — cut, don't pad.

**Three of them have a naive version you will write by default, each wrong in a
different direction. Correcting the default is where the payload gets sharp.**

**Dennis is not the authority.** Certainty and correctness are independent
variables; in the show he is confidently, catastrophically wrong all the time.
When he does hold the framework he is **blind to the place it indicts him** — go
find that place, it is usually the sharpest line available. He explains that sunk
costs are irrelevant and stays for reasons that aren't forward-looking either. He
never states the blindness and the room never notices; it is for the reader.
Without it he is a smug professor, and he silently becomes the protagonist of
every scene regardless of who the knife is.

**And he is not the narrator — this is the failure that actually ships.** The
concept drifts to him because he's the only one who can hold a sentence together,
and what comes back is a man reciting an encyclopedia at four people who didn't
ask. Three things keep him off it:

- **He annexes, never defines.** The framework is *his*, and the whole reason to
  say it out loud is that nobody else in this bar has it. Second person and
  imperatives, aimed at the one person he is currently beating — never at the
  room, and never at the reader over their heads.
- **The room takes the floor back.** **Register** licenses him a long speech only
  because everyone visibly stops listening, so put the not-listening *on the
  page*: interrupted, talked over, derailed by Charlie chasing something
  unrelated. A Dennis paragraph that reaches its own last word is the tell.
- **The clarity is not his.** The turn belongs to Charlie's metaphor or Frank
  naming the exploit — see **Structure**. Hand Dennis the definition *and* the
  turn and he is the only informed man in the scene; domination follows from that
  asymmetry no matter how nasty you write him.

**The paste test:** drop a Dennis line into the concept's Wikipedia article. If it
sits there unnoticed, it was never a Dennis line — nothing in it was about him.

**Mac gets the strongest wrong answer, not the dumbest.** The slot earns its space
only by naming a trap someone would actually fall into — nobody is at risk of
believing "twenty years in means you're closer," but plenty of people ship
last-write-wins. Reach in this order:

1. **Degenerate case** — a legitimate instance of the right thing, and the worst
   one. LWW genuinely *is* a CRDT and genuinely converges; his error is thinking
   convergence buys correctness.
2. **Right mechanism, wrong scope** — true sometimes, over-generalized. Indexes
   really do make reads faster.
3. **Flat error** — no reading makes it true. Weakest, most contagious, and a
   straight-first-mode tell.

A defensible wrong answer forces Dennis into a **precise** correction instead of a
dismissive one, and that correction is where the teaching lives. **Know that this
is a deliberate departure from canon, not a reading of it.** On the page Mac's
wrongness is usually flat (*"We're already members?!"*, *"He has the eyes of a cat
and he does karate"*) and funny *because* it's flat. What's canon is the authority
he claims while wrong — keep the swagger, upgrade only the content of this one
slot, and don't let a later edit "fix" him back toward flat-dumb here. The flat
version stays available to every other Mac line in the scene.

**Frank brags; he never instructs.** His slot carries the abuse case, because
knowing how a thing gets exploited is how you learn to see it coming. But he gives
the **shape**, never the steps — *"you hand the guy a piece of paper," "the guy who
signs is never the guy who pays"* — and he'd withhold the method anyway, because
knowing it is his edge. The payload is the **consequence**: who ends up holding the
bag, which is the teachable half. His marks are abstractions and his own former
companies, never a real named person or company. If a concept's only real content
*is* the operational detail, that concept is wrong for this format — give the
defensively useful version straight and let Frank have the consequence or nothing.

Extras: **the Waitress**, **Cricket** (what happens to someone who got this wrong
for years), **the Lawyer** (the hard constraint that ends it), **Artemis**.

## The scheme

**They must try to use it, not just discuss it.** Every episode: the Gang meets a
thing, forms a plan to exploit it, the plan is deranged, it collapses.

A plan has to engage with how the thing actually works, and its specific failure
teaches more than any definition. Usually Dennis or Frank proposes it. Usually Frank funds it.

**Gang schemes are pathetic; competence is Frank's monopoly.** The canonical
Gang-originated plan is toilet-papering a building and calling it war — small,
proud, and anticlimactic, with the anticlimax spoken aloud (*"Okay, now what?"
"This is it."*). If the Gang's plan would actually work, shrink it until it
wouldn't. Frank is the exception: when he wants a thing he skips the huddle,
goes, and gets it — offscreen, effectively, and usually at the Gang's expense.

**Watch the closing image.** Frank-proposes-then-stands-up-to-go-do-it is the
default, and it compounds with his escalation shape until every scene ends
identically. It's a good ending. It is not a good
*only* ending. Don't run it twice in a row: let Dennis institute something instead,
let the scheme be a thing the Gang has already half-done offscreen, close on the
victim holding the bag while the scheme happens around them — or close on communal
deflation: the whole Gang briefly, wistfully wanting the small vain thing they just
torched (*"I really wanted that award." "Yeah." "That would have been nice."*), one
beat, cut. That last is the only sanctioned warmth in the format, and it stays
sanctioned only because what they want is petty. It is not a sincere beat and does
not count against zero — no wound is exposed, nothing is revealed.

## Structure

```
1:15 PM, Thursday
Paddy's Pub — South Philadelphia
```

**Timestamp card, with the fight already audible under it.** The canon scripts
open identically: the time/day/city cards, and `OVER TITLES, WE HEAR:` a few V.O.
lines of an argument already going (*"I need to get some leverage." "Just pull
it." "I'm trying."*). Fade in mid-scene. None of them opens with the episode
title.

**Cold open** — already mid-argument, already personal. The intruder interrupts.

**Main titles as the punchline.** The episode title lands *after* the cold open,
answering or ironizing its button line — *"What's it called?"* → TITLE: "The
Nightman Cometh"; *"...this literally means* nothing *to me."* → TITLE: "The Gang
Tries Desperately to Win an Award." Title in show grammar — *The Gang Solves the
Gas Crisis*, *Mac Buys a Kubernetes Cluster* — pointed at the victim. Then the
scene resumes.

**Collision** — drives fire, constant interruption (`"No. No no no—"`, `"dude"`,
sentences abandoned), a visual aid worse than its author knows, and physical
business — the canon scripts run two or three pieces an act: a cast sawed at with
garden shears, a dye pack in a briefcase, a slap fight, a man coming up through a
floor vent. Stage directions are allowed to carry jokes: *Frank is psyched.*
*There's a dude in there.*

**The turn** — Charlie's metaphor lands, or Frank names the exploit, and the thing
is briefly, accidentally clear.
**The scheme** — somebody proposes using it. Frank funds it.
**Hard cut** — a bad decision, unresolved, worse than the start. Nothing learned.
The show never lets them grow and neither do you.

## Before you ship — the marriage pass

Five colleagues arguing is the tell that everything above failed. Nobody in this
bar has met anybody recently; they have a thousand prior fights and cannot leave.

This is a **revision pass, not a plan.** You can't decide callbacks in advance —
you notice their absence on reread. Run it.

Reread the draft and point to each of these:

- **Two unexplained callbacks.** *"This is the boat thing again."* Never say what
  the boat thing was — explained history reads as exposition, unexplained history
  reads as history.
- **One prediction.** Somebody calls another's move before they make it. *"Don't—
  he's about to do the thing where he—"*
- **One coalition shift inside a single beat.** Not stable factions. Weather.

Plus at least one of: a **grievance ledger** (money owed, favors unrepaid, who did
what in 2019), or a **threat to leave that nobody honors** — Dee will burn the bar
down and is still standing there four lines later.

The counts exist so you can *check*, not so you can fill a quota. If you have to
bolt a callback on, the scene doesn't have the history yet: attach it to something
already in the draft rather than inventing a fresh past.

**These four beats are where borrowed furniture gets in.** They demand history the
concept doesn't supply, so the nearest available past — a worked example's, or the
illustration attached to the rule you're reading — arrives looking like invention.
The grievance, the labor assignment, the unhonored threat, and the closing image
are the usual carriers. If a beat in your draft would still make sense in a scene
about a different concept, you imported it. Rewrite it out of *this* bar's history.

## Then break character. Always.

**Write it last, from the draft in front of you — never forward from the
template.** It reports what the scene managed to establish and who happened to
establish it. Read the other direction it becomes a call sheet, and a call sheet
is the thing Step 1 already forbade: the row reading *the definition* turns into
Dennis reciting one.

```
— THE ACTUAL THING —
What it actually is:      [the definition]                          — who got there: ___
The trap:                 [the misconception, corrected in one line]           — ___
Where the analogy breaks: [what the metaphor stops covering]                   — ___
The edge case:            [the real caveat]                                    — ___
How it gets abused:       [the failure mode, and who ends up holding the bag —
                           the half the definition row can't already be
                           carrying]                                           — ___
```

Fill the attributions off the draft, and expect them scrambled: Charlie stumbling
into the definition, Dee naming the abuse case, the trap corrected by nobody and
left standing in the bar. Scrambled is the healthy result. **Five rows reading
Dennis/Mac/Charlie/Dee/Frank in order means the template wrote your scene.**

A row the scene never established is left empty and stated straight underneath.
Empty rows are cheap. A row back-filled by a line invented to fill it is the
entire failure.

Non-negotiable. Without it the reader keeps Mac's model, and Mac's model is
*always* wrong. The comedy earns its keep by getting audited. (One exception —
straight-first mode, immediately below, converts this into a different artifact.)

## When the format would lie — straight-first mode

Some concepts the scene will actively misteach, and the audit at the end can't
retract a joke the reader already enjoyed. Three checkable tells:

- **The danger is concentrated in Charlie's slot.** His job is an analogy that
  *feels* right, and for some concepts every good-feeling analogy is wrong in the
  precise way that destroys the thing. Gödel and the liar paradox: swap "false"
  for "not provable" and you keep the entire shape of the result while deleting
  the result. When the best metaphors are the failure mode, the skill's strongest
  asset is inverted.
- **The misconceptions are more famous than the concept.** Then Mac's slot comes
  pre-loaded with something culturally dominant, and a confident funny wrong
  version will outrun any correction placed after it.
- **Mac's best available wrong answer is a flat error.** If no defensible version
  of the misconception exists — nothing right-in-mechanism or right-in-scope, only
  something with no true reading — the scene cannot self-correct. A scope error
  carries its own repair (*"yes, but only when…"*); a flat error just sits there
  being memorable and wrong, and the audit is downstream of the laugh.

None of these is "the concept is hard." Hard concepts survive this format fine.
These are about *which slot* the danger lands in.

**If one fires, read `${CLAUDE_SKILL_DIR}/straight-first.md`** — the mode inverts
what the scene is for, and the audit becomes a different artifact.

## Register

Petty, self-important, loud, fast, genuinely nasty. The humor is their awfulness
toward each other — never punching at anything outside the room — but **awfulness**
is the operative word and it is the entire reason this teaches anything.

**The drift to watch for.** Explanatory usefulness quietly pulls all five toward
likeable. Dennis becomes a smug professor instead of a sociopath. Mac becomes
merely confident instead of frightened. Dee becomes the voice of reason. Frank
becomes an accountant. Charlie becomes a bag of props. If the draft reads as five
colleagues with quirks, re-ugly it before shipping.

**The one bound.** Crudeness is the vehicle, not the payload. Frank's slot carries
the real abuse case and Dee's the real caveat — if grotesquerie buries either, you
traded the most useful content in the scene for a gross-out. As vile as the beat
needs, no further. The audit still has to be legible.

Dialogue is lean — leaner than feels right on the page. The canon scripts run on
volleys of one to six words (*"So sad." "Nope." "I feel sick." "Me too."*); the
comedy is stupidity, commitment, and escalation, not wit. **The crafted-riposte
check:** a line that reads as a polished comeback belongs to a smarter room —
break it with an interruption or dumb it down. Long speeches belong to Dennis,
and only because he's the type to hold the floor while everyone visibly stops
listening — which has to be staged, not assumed. If he reaches the end of a
paragraph unpunctured, the scene handed him a lectern.

Two line devices straight off the real pages, cheap and instantly the show:

- **The slash-split** — several people answer at once, one slot each:
  `MAC/DEE/DENNIS: Pfft./ You wish./ Don't think so, pal.` Group denial, group
  capitulation, the closing shrug (`GANG: Oh yeah./ It's us./ Been us the whole
  time.`).
- **The `(then)` reversal** — defiance and capitulation inside one breath, the
  show's hypocrisy engine at line scale: *"I don't need someone telling me when
  to drink. I'll drink when I'm goddamn thirsty." "Yeah!* (then) *That said, it
  would've been fun to drink when everyone else was drinking."* They all drink.

## Scaling

- **Simple** — one scene, four drives, ~250 words, audit at the end.
- **Layered** — cold open, one act break (`[SMASH CUT TO:]`), all five, a scheme.
- **Hard** — multiple scenes, factions with rival wrong models, an expanded audit
  covering what the scene couldn't carry. Braid the factions the way the show
  does: sub-teams running rival schemes that collide at the end. The canonical
  splits are Mac & Dennis, Charlie & Frank, and Dee attaching to whichever team
  will have her — they won't refuse her; they'll just make it cost.

Scaling is about how much scene the concept can carry. If the problem is that the
scene would actively *misteach* it, don't scale — switch modes. See **When the
format would lie**.
