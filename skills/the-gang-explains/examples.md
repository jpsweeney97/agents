# Worked examples

Two complete runs. Read one before drafting if you want calibration on length,
density, or how hard the register should hit. Each is preceded by the decisions
that produced it — the decisions are the transferable part; the scene is proof
they were made.

**Take the decisions, not the furniture.** Measured behaviour, not a worry: a run
on a concept *adjacent* to one of these will re-import its grievance beat, its
labor assignment, its threat-and-return, and its closing image, and none of it
will feel like copying at the time. Frank puts a sandwich down in both of these;
he does not have to. Nothing below is canon — no prop, callback, or closing shape
here is part of the format, and a scene reusing one is a scene with no history of
its own.

Line economy has been passed against the real scripts: composed comebacks broken
or dumbed down per the crafted-riposte check in SKILL.md's Register. Lines that
still run long are payload slots or Dennis holding the floor, both licensed.

## Example 1 — standard mode

**Concept:** survivorship bias
**Knife:** Charlie
**Sincere beat:** one, taken
**Scale:** layered

**Knife selection.** The reader's first reach is Dee — auditioning is right there
in the knife list, and she counts only the people who made it. That is exactly
why she's wrong: the reader already made that joke. Charlie is the least obvious
viable one, at one step: survivorship bias is about the data that isn't there
because it didn't survive to be recorded, and Charlie is the uncounted labor this
bar runs on. He is the missing plane. He doesn't understand the concept and it
never touches him on camera — knife by resonance, which is the good kind.

**Payload collision.** Dee and Mac both wanted the same insight here — Dee counts
only working actors, Mac counts only answered prayers, and both arrive at "you're
only looking at the winners." Two characters reaching for one payload is not a
problem to design around; give it to whoever gets there *first* and make the
other one's failure to get there the joke. Dee lands it, Mac tries to annex it,
and the annexation is funnier than the insight was.

**Closing image.** Not Frank-stands-up. Closed on the victim holding the bag —
literally, under the visual aid.

**Title card.** After Dee's "watch him name it with letters" — the main-titles
card is the show naming it with letters, which is the answer-the-button shape.

```
— THE ACTUAL THING —
Dennis was right about:   Survivorship bias is conditioning on the outcome. When
                          you select a sample by survival and then search it for
                          the cause of survival, every shared trait you find is
                          uninformative until you check the dead ones for it too.
Mac was wrong about:      A bigger sample. Enlarging a sample that was selected on
                          the outcome makes you more confident, not more correct —
                          a hundred surviving bars is the same bias, tighter.
Charlie's metaphor holds: You can't tell "tough" from "lucky" by looking only at
                          the glasses still on the shelf; you need the bag.
                          Where it breaks: sometimes survival really is caused by
                          the trait, and the survivors really are tougher. The bias
                          isn't that survivors are unremarkable — it's that looking
                          only at survivors can't distinguish the two cases.
Dee's caveat:             The fix is usually not "also study the failures,"
                          because the failures are structurally unreachable —
                          closed bars have nobody to answer the phone, dead funds
                          get delisted, the planes that didn't come back are on
                          the seabed. When the missing data is missing by
                          construction you have to reason about the selection
                          mechanism instead of collecting more.
Frank's exploit:          The split-list prediction scam. Mail both calls to
                          halves of a list, drop the half you got wrong, repeat.
                          The last cohort has watched you be right six times
                          running and will pay for the system. Any performance
                          record that only reports the entities still operating
                          is the same trick with better manners.
```

## Example 2 — straight-first mode

**Concept:** p-values / statistical significance
**Mode:** straight-first, all three tells fire
**Knife:** Dennis
**Sincere beat:** zero
**Scale:** layered

**Why the mode fires.**

1. *Danger concentrated in Charlie's slot* — every intuitive analogy for a
   p-value inverts the conditional. "How likely is it I'm wrong" is the shape
   the good-feeling metaphor keeps producing, and it is the exact error.
2. *Misconceptions more famous than the concept* — "95% chance it's real" is
   culturally dominant and would outrun any correction placed after it.
3. *Mac's best wrong answer is a flat error* — "5% chance it was a fluke" has no
   true reading. Nothing right-in-scope is available, so the scene cannot
   self-correct.

**Knife selection.** Dennis, at one step: measurement and evaluation, and the
machinery for asking *or did that just happen?* aimed at a man who needs to be
exceptional. Worth noting because it looks like a violation: Dennis normally
centers every scene by owning the correct-definition slot, so making him the
knife too would double down. Straight-first mode displaces him from that slot —
the straight explanation already carried the definition — so this is the one mode
where Dennis-as-victim doesn't over-center.

**Closing image.** Dennis alone, colder than the start. Dennis's sincere beats are
horror rather than pathos, and the same is true of his endings.

**Title card.** After "it means the script *works*" — the title ironizes the
button: he has, in the only sense available, achieved significance.

---

**Straight first — the scene would misteach this one, so here is the actual thing
before anybody talks.**

A p-value is the probability of seeing data **at least as extreme as yours, if
the null hypothesis were true.** It is P(data | hypothesis). The number everyone
actually wants — the probability the effect is real, given the data — is
P(hypothesis | data). That is a different number and you cannot get it without a
prior you didn't supply. So: p < 0.05 is not 95% confidence, is not a 5% chance
of a fluke, and says nothing whatsoever about how big the effect is.

Now the scene, which is dessert.

**MAC:** *(not asking)* Mm.

**DENNIS:** It means the gap isn't noise, Mac. It means the script *works.*

**[MAIN TITLES]**

### "Dennis Achieves Significance"

**MAC:** So there's a ninety-six percent—
**DENNIS:** No.
**MAC:** —ninety-six percent chance it's real, that's a—
**DENNIS:** *No.* Mac. Everybody says that. Everybody says it *instantly* — you
didn't think, it just fell out of you. It is not the chance it's real. It is the
chance I'd see a gap this big **if the script did nothing at all.**
**MAC:** Same numbers though.
**DENNIS:** Not the same—
**MAC:** They're sitting right next to each other on the—
**DENNIS:** They point in *opposite directions*, Mac. One of them is about the
*world.* One of them is about the *data.* You cannot flip them around, that's not
a — you can't just—
**DEE:** He's gonna say significant.
**DENNIS:** —it is **significant**—
**DEE:** There it is.

*CHARLIE, halfway under the bar sink with a wrench. He backs out holding it.*

**CHARLIE:** Okay so it's like — okay, if I say the pipes are fine, right? And
then there's water all over the floor. The p-value is like how much of a *liar* I
am.
**DENNIS:** No. That's Mac's thing again.
**CHARLIE:** It's not Mac's thing, I said *liar*—
**DENNIS:** It's Mac's thing. You made it wet. You're both asking how likely the
*claim* is, given the water. Nobody gets that out of the water. You'd have to
already know how many pipes in this building were shot before you went down there.
**CHARLIE:** *(long pause, genuinely working)* ...So it's how *weird the water is*
if the pipes really are fine.
**DENNIS:** *(quietly furious)* ...Yes.
**CHARLIE:** That's what I said.
**DENNIS:** That is emphatically not what you said.
**MAC:** He said liar.
**CHARLIE:** I said liar but I *meant* the water.
**MAC:** *(to DENNIS)* He meant the water. I think he had it.
**CHARLIE:** I had it!
**DENNIS:** You did *not* have it, you arrived at it, there's a—
**MAC:** He *had* it, dude.

*DEE, from the taps, not looking up.*

**DEE:** How big's the gap.
**DENNIS:** It's significant.
**DEE:** That's not what I asked you. How *big.*
**DENNIS:** Four points.
**DEE:** Four p— on how many people?
**DENNIS:** Four hundred.
**DEE:** So you ran a script at four hundred women, moved a number by four
percent, and you're calling that a *discovery.*
**DENNIS:** It's significant, Dee.
**DEE:** Significant doesn't mean *big*, you absolute— you can get a tiny little
nothing to be significant, you just have to bother enough people—
**DENNIS:** Says the woman who went on twelve auditions, booked one, and calls it

**DEE:** That is—
**DENNIS:** Twelve swings, Dee! Take enough swings, one connects. It means
**nothing.** That's not a career. That's *math.*
**MAC:** *(to DENNIS)* How many versions of the script did you run?
**DENNIS:** Eleven. Locked the twelfth.
**MAC:** Cool.

*FRANK puts the sandwich down.*

**FRANK:** This is money.
**DENNIS:** It's a *finding*, Frank.
**FRANK:** It's money. You got a piece of paper that says a thing works. You don't

**DEE:** That's fraud.
**FRANK:** It's a *study.*
**DENNIS:** ...That's not how research works.
**FRANK:** Kid, that is *exactly* how research works. It's got a name and

**MAC:** We could do a seminar. A seminar with a *slide.*
**DEE:** I'm not standing next to you at a seminar.
**MAC:** You'd be *on* the slide.
**DEE:** ...What's on the slide.
**FRANK:** You still owe me four hundred bucks from the clipboard thing.
**DEE:** That was Charlie!
**CHARLIE:** That was me.
**FRANK:** I know.

**DENNIS:** *(to nobody)* Twelve.

**[HARD CUT]**

```
— WHAT THE SCENE GETS WRONG ON PURPOSE —
Mac's "ninety-six percent"   — p is P(data this extreme | null true), never
                               P(null true | data). Without a prior there is no
                               such number to report.
Charlie's first swing        — inverted the conditional, which is the error every
                               intuitive analogy for this produces. It is staged
                               on purpose. His second swing is correct: "how weird
                               the water is if the pipes really are fine" is the
                               definition.
Dennis's eleven variants     — he demolishes Dee for exactly this and does not
                               notice. Eleven independent tests at .05 give you a
                               43% chance of at least one "significant" result
                               when nothing works at all.
Dee                          — is not misreading. p says nothing about effect
                               size, and a large enough n will make a trivial
                               effect significant. Her question was the right one.
Frank                        — is not misreading. Run it until it comes out right
                               and publish the one that did is the entire abuse
                               surface, and it does have a name: p-hacking.
```
