# Decision Flip

An analysis can be wrong two ways: leaning on a number that could honestly swing its conclusion, or polishing numbers that cannot change it. The flip test catches both — it finds the input whose honest uncertainty actually matters, and it licenses stopping when none does. This is not option-scoring: no invented weights, no manufactured probabilities — only the analysis's own numbers, moved within ranges committed before any flip is computed.

## The moves

1. **Pin the conclusion and its flip line.** The claim as stated, and what exactly would count as it changing — a sign flips, a threshold is crossed, a ranking reverses, a go/no-go bar is missed. If no flip line can be articulated, the analysis isn't concluding anything falsifiable; say so and stop.
2. **Inventory the load-bearing estimates.** Every number the conclusion leans on that is estimated, assumed, or extrapolated — including the silent ones (denominators, conversion rates, "we assumed linear"). Measured facts and contractual constants are excluded: they have no honest range to move within.
3. **Pre-register honest ranges — before any flip math.** For each estimate, the range you would actually defend, with its basis: measured nearby → tight; base-rated from a reference class → that class's spread (outside-view sets this, where available); gut → wide, and marked as gut. Commit every range before computing a single flip. A range chosen after seeing its effect is gerrymandered.
4. **Flip-test one input at a time.** Push each estimate to both ends of its range while the others hold at their stated values; record flips or holds, showing the arithmetic so the result is checkable. Where two inputs plausibly move together, test the pair at their plausible joint extreme and say why that pairing is plausible — but build no simulation; this is words and arithmetic.
5. **Render the stability verdict — one of three:**
   - **Robust.** No honest range flips it. The conclusion stands across your actual uncertainty; further estimate-polishing is waste. Decide and move — this verdict is a license to stop.
   - **One crux.** Exactly one input flips it. State its break-even value — the point in that input's own units where the conclusion turns — so the crux becomes a single askable question ("do we believe at least 19 clients adopt?"). Attach the cheapest probe that would narrow that one range: a data pull, a question to whoever owns the number, a reference-class check, a small pilot. Everything else is settled enough; chase nothing but the crux.
   - **Estimate-driven.** Several inputs flip it independently. The numbers cannot carry the conclusion, and tighter-looking arithmetic would be costume: say so plainly. The honest next step is better data on the named inputs, or an explicit values-and-risk call by the decision's owner — not more precision theater.

## The close

The stability verdict is about the conclusion, never about the decision — robust does not mean right, and the decision stays with its owner. Show the flip line, the pre-registered ranges with their bases, and the arithmetic, so the whole packet is contestable. Close with one honest line naming the range you are least sure of — the flip test is only as honest as its widest gerrymander.
