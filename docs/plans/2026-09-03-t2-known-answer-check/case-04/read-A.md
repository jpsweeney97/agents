Best: **Introduce a preventive-maintenance schedule**
Why: The cause of the jump from three lost days to fourteen is not known, so the best option is one that reduces unplanned stops whatever the cause, and this is the only option in the field that lowers how often the press stops without betting on one cause. It is done by the two trained operators, so it satisfies the insurance constraint, spends no capital, and needs no stoppage beyond routine checks. Weekly checks over the ten weeks before peak will catch wear on the parts most likely to fail during October to December, and the record the operators keep will show the pattern behind the recent stops. It puts the operators in charge of the machine, which keeps their confidence in it, and it creates no dependence on the manufacturer's contract. The options that shorten each stop (spares, repair training) are good complements, but they leave the failure rate unchanged, and reliability through peak is the value the user ranked first.
Runners-up, in order: **Stock spare parts for the common failures**, **Train the operators in fault repair**, **Run the press at reduced speed**
Unknowns that would change the choice: If the fourteen days were two or three long waits for parts or a technician rather than many short stops, stocking spares would move to first. If the shop already knows the single cause of the recent stops (for example air supply, electrical, or paper feed), the matching targeted option would beat a general schedule. If the manufacturer's repair course is short and cheap and one operator can attend while the other runs the press, training would rise to a close second.
