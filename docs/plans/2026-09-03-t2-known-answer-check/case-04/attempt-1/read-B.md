Best: **Replace the shop's compressed-air dryer**
Why: E1 shows ten of the fourteen lost days were gripper and pneumatic-valve faults, and E2, E3, and E4 all trace those faults to one cause: the compressed-air dryer failed in January, was bypassed, and moisture in the air lines has been destroying gripper cylinders and valves ever since the weather turned humid. E2 (the manufacturer's own technician) says replacement parts will keep failing until air drying is restored, and E3 confirms it: the $1,900 of spares stocked in June were used up by August. The fix costs $3,200, takes one day, and involves no work on the press itself (E3), so it clears all three hard constraints, which rule out the press purchase, refurbishment, controller, climate control, and a hired technician (E5, E8). It fits every stated value: it restores reliability at the cause, it does not add a vendor contract, and E2 and E4 give the operators a documented reason to trust the machine again. The remaining stops are baseline feeder misfeeds (E9) and one fuse with no power events logged (E1), so the electrical upgrade, paper-supplier change, and reduced speed (E6 shows faults continued at half speed) do not address what is actually failing.
Runners-up, in order: **Stock spare parts for the common failures**, **Introduce a preventive-maintenance schedule**, **Train the operators in fault repair**
Unknowns that would change the choice: Whether the failed dryer is the only source of moisture, since E4 requires a pressure dew point of 3 °C or lower and nothing in the evidence confirms the quoted replacement reaches it. Whether the air-supply contractor can fit the one-day job inside the ten-week window; the quote in E3 is from January and may be stale. Whether the cylinders and valves already damaged by moisture will keep failing after the air is dried, which would make stocking spares a necessary companion rather than a runner-up.
