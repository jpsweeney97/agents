# Prune: the permit backlog

Survivor count asked for: about four. Survivors: four. No cut was blocked by an unpriced value trade. Fifteen options in the field; eleven cut records below.

## Survivors (field order, exact wordings)

1. **Authorize overtime for reviewers** (user's) — "Fund overtime for the review staff until the backlog clears."
2. **Reassign reviewers from commercial permits** — Move staff from the commercial-permit team to residential for the season.
3. **Create a fast lane for simple applications** — Route minor work (decks, fences, small additions) to an expedited path.
4. **Change which applications get the supervisor sign-off** — Route only some application types through the supervisor sign-off that follows the three reviews, rather than all of them.

Notes for the later stages, not grounds for any cut:

- "Authorize overtime for reviewers" spends reviewer morale, a stated value the user has not priced against speed.
- "Reassign reviewers from commercial permits" passes constraint 2 because it moves existing staff; whether the commercial reviewers hold the qualifications for residential zoning, structural, and fire review is not stated and should be confirmed.
- "Create a fast lane for simple applications" departs from first come, first served, a stated value the user has not priced against speed. It reorders the queue; it does not add reviewer-hours.
- "Change which applications get the supervisor sign-off" passes constraint 1 because the sign-off follows the three legally required reviews and is not one of them. Whether the sign-off is itself required by ordinance is not stated and should be confirmed. Its value turns entirely on whether the sign-off step is a bottleneck, which cannot be resolved at sketch depth; it survives because a single step that every application passes through after fourteen reviewers' output is a plausible one.

## Cut records (field order)

```text

Cut:            constraint, fact-established
Reason:         Fails confirmed constraint 2 (no new staff or contractors before the next budget cycle). Temporary staff need a council vote that takes eight of the ten weeks available; the user's cost line says adding people is out.
Strongest case: Qualified extra hands are the most direct answer to a shortfall in reviewer-hours, and it leaves the process and the permanent headcount untouched. Even if the vote were already under way, two weeks of temporary capacity would clear part of the backlog.
Revive if:      The council vote is already scheduled or waived, or the next budget cycle arrives before the winter cutoff.
```

```text
Option:         Run the three reviews concurrently instead of in sequence
Cut:            survivor count, judgment call
Reason:         Low-confidence cut of a candidate whose seriousness I could not resolve at sketch depth. What I can see: it adds no reviewer-hours and shortens no review, so completions per week stay bounded by the slowest of the three review stages whether the stages run in sequence or at once. On that reading it shortens each application's elapsed time once the queue is gone but does not shrink the 1,100 now pending, and it can add work, since a sequence stops at a failed zoning review while concurrency does all three regardless. That reading is a sketch-depth argument, not an established fact.
Strongest case: The field calls it the standard throughput fix for a sequential workflow. It needs no new people, no budget line, and no departure from first come, first served. If applications sit idle between stages, or downstream reviewers wait on upstream output, concurrency recovers that wasted time at once, and elapsed days per application is exactly what the forty-five-day law measures.
Revive if:      Reviewers for any of the three stages are observed idle while applications wait in hand-off, or the office's own data shows that time between stages, not review time, is where the thirty-four days go.
```

```text
Option:         Batch similar applications for review
Cut:            same reason, judgment call
Reason:         Stands or falls with "Create a fast lane for simple applications" for the same reason: both change the order applications are handled in, grouping by type, and both depend on the office accepting a departure from first come, first served to gain throughput. If the office refuses to reorder, both die; if it accepts, the fast lane is the same move applied where it pays most (the many small applications), with the added effect of returning small jobs to their construction season. Kept the fast lane; neither is the user's candidate.

Revive if:      The office accepts reordering and complex applications, not simple ones, are where reviewer time goes; then batching the complex types is the version of the move that helps, and it can run alongside the fast lane.
```
